**1. Why did car use barely change after the first six months?**

A. The bicycles were too expensive to rent.  
B. The scheme mainly replaced walking and bus journeys.  
C. Residents had not heard about the service.  
D. Shopping centres refused to provide parking.

**2. What discouraged commuters from switching to bicycles?**

A. The lack of safe routes to their workplaces.  
B. The distance between shops and railway stations.  
C. The twenty-minute limit on all journeys.  
D. The poor quality of the bicycles.

**3. What did the council do in the second stage?**

A. It removed all parking spaces in the town.  
B. It increased the number of bicycles.  
C. It introduced a protected route linked to the station.  
D. It required businesses to subsidise rentals.

**4. What does the speaker imply about the reported business effects?**

A. Higher spending per visit explains the results.  
B. The results prove that parking never matters.  
C. The results are irrelevant to transport policy.  
D. More frequent visits may compensate for smaller purchases.

**5. Which conclusion is best supported?**

A. Popularity alone does not establish a reduction in traffic.  
B. A single successful trial justifies a town-wide expansion.  
C. Survey responses are always more reliable than trip counts.  
D. Weather has no influence on transport choices.

> [!success]- 完成后展开：答案与证据
> **1 B · 2 A · 3 C · 4 D · 5 A**  
> 1：原来步行、坐公交的人改骑车，属于交通方式替代，并未明显减少驾驶。  
> 2：便利不等于安全；转折 but 后指出 routes felt unsafe。  
> 3：不是继续买车，而是建设保护车道并连到车站。  
> 4：单次购买少，但频次更高；注意 even though 的让步关系。  
> 5：trip counts 衡量使用量，不能单独说明汽车行程减少。  
> 局限：天气异常、志愿者样本偏差、观察期不足。错题复盘须记录支持答案的原句，不只记字母。

> [!quote]- 听力原文：请先完成盲听
> Good morning. Today I would like to discuss an experiment carried out in the fictional town of Brookfield. When the local council introduced a bicycle-sharing scheme, officials expected traffic to fall almost immediately. They placed hundreds of bicycles near shopping centres and offered the first twenty minutes of every journey free of charge. After six months, however, car use had hardly changed.
>
> A survey provided a possible explanation. Most people using the bicycles had previously walked or taken the bus. People who drove to work said that the bicycles themselves were convenient, but the routes to their offices felt unsafe. Providing a new service had therefore changed how existing public transport users travelled, without removing many cars from the road.
>
> The council then tried a different approach. Rather than buying more bicycles, it converted a short section of a busy road into a protected cycling lane and connected it to the railway station. Businesses worried that losing parking spaces would reduce sales. Yet interviews later suggested that customers arriving by bicycle visited more frequently, even though they spent less on each visit.
>
> These findings should be interpreted cautiously. The trial took place during unusually dry weather, and volunteers who answered the surveys may have been more enthusiastic about cycling than other residents. The researchers recommended comparing several neighbourhoods over a full year before expanding the scheme.
>
> The lesson is not that bicycle sharing fails. It is that success depends on the problem being measured. Counting trips tells us whether a service is popular. To discover whether it reduces traffic, we must also ask which journeys those trips replace.
